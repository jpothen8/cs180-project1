In this project I aim to form the Prokudin-Gorskii glass plate images into a single color image by aligning the captured intensities of the same subject under different colored glass. This wont result in perfect results as there is slight variation between the 3 images, but the goal is to minimize the amount of visual artifacts through strategies below and with trial and error.

All reconstructions align green to blue and red to green, then add the shifts to place red relative to blue. Offsets are applied to green and red relative to blue, in (x, y) pixels. Positive x moves right; positive y moves down.

Single-scale alignment

NCC and L2 · G→B and R→G · search ±15 pixels · scoring border 15%

Since each scan of the subject contains three grayscale exposures of the same scene under different colored glass panes, the exposures will appear at slightly different positions due to variability in taking the image, and stacking them immediately will cause a lot of visual artifacts in colored outlines or doubled objects. We assume that we can fix these visual artifacts with a translation only, so just moving the entire channel or these images up and down and left and right, and we're not doing any rotation. For this project, we're using a search from negative 15 to positive 15 pixels, so we can shift each channel by this amount. We also exclude the outer 15% of each channel because the borders can sometimes dominate the comparison without looking at the actual important part, which is the scene.

A and B are the reference and shifted patches over the same interior pixels Ω. μ A and μ B are their means; N is the number of pixels.

L2 distance

L2(A, B) = √ ∑ p∈Ω (A p − B p ) 2

Minimize L2. Code score: −L2²/N (maximize).

For this strategy, we will take the L2 norm between the images produced by these different color glass panes, and we'll try to align the image to minimize the intensity difference across each corresponding pixel between these channels. So essentially, we compare green to blue and red to green separately. For each possible shift, we calculate the mean squared brightness difference between corresponding pixels in the cropped region and choose the shift with the smallest value. This selects the same alignment as minimizing the L2 norm.

Normalized cross-correlation

NCC(A, B) = ∑ p∈Ω (A p − μ A )(B p − μ B ) √ ∑ p∈Ω (A p − μ A ) 2 √ ∑ p∈Ω (B p − μ B ) 2

Maximize NCC.

With NCC, the main problem we're trying to solve is difference in varying brightness levels across these color channels. So essentially, we're trying to say, can we try to find a metric that will make sure that the patterns across the color channels are as similar as possible? The benefit of this is that it doesn't penalize two color channels for being different just because of their intensity values. For example, if the red channel is the exact same as the green channel in its particular shift, but all of its intensities are doubled, most likely we would want to keep that shift even though the L2 norm will have a high error for that. So how does NCC work? Essentially, we compare the entire cropped image that we are testing against. We subtract the mean brightness of each cropped image, multiply the corresponding mean-centered values, and add them up. We then divide this sum by the product of the two mean-centered images’ L2 norms. Subtracting the means accounts for overall brightness differences, while dividing by the norms accounts for contrast differences. So for example, let's say if we have an array of 10, 20, and 30, and 40, 60, and 80, similar to my example from before, the L2 error will be a lot, but the NCC score will be 1, because after we normalize both these images, we notice that they have the exact same pattern. We start off on a number, and then after demeaning both of these, we get -10, 0, 10, and -20, 0, 20, respectively, which both share the exact same contrast pattern. Because of this, we get the maximum NCC score, which means strong agreement, and because of this normalization, we know that NCC will range from negative 1 to 1.

Multi-scale alignment

Raw-pixel NCC · G→B and R→G · coarsest side ≤200 pixels · coarse search ±15 · refinement ±2

We run into a problem with this earlier two approaches with larger images because of two reasons. First, we need larger searching areas. We need much more than 15 pixels, and this means that this increases the computational complexity of our searching problem. And then on top of that, our images are also bigger, so finding our loss will also be more computationally expensive. So the core idea is that working with these large images is a pretty big hassle. So what we can do is try to downsample these images into a smaller resolution and then find the best displacement for these downsampled images, and then gradually get back to our original quality, so we can find the exact shift needed for this image. In my project, I create smaller images by applying the kernel [1, 2, 1] divided by 4, both horizontally and vertically, and then keeping every second pixel. Because this is a low-pass filter, it will smooth out fine detail before sampling, and without smoothing, these fine patterns can actually turn into visual artifacts called aliasing. I stop downsampling when the longest side is at most 200 pixels, and we do our first alignment with that lowest resolution image with a plus minus 15 pixel search in that pixel space. As we move to each larger level, we double the previously estimated shift and search within plus or minus 2 pixels of that estimate. We double the shift because each image dimension approximately doubles between levels. The main flaw of this algorithm is that an incorrect coarse estimate at the very beginning can lead later searches into the wrong region, and it may never reach the optimal alignment.

Emir: why I used red to green

Red via green: R→B = R→G + G→B. All four examples use a 15% scoring border and a coarsest side of 200 px.

Why did I used red to green and green to blue as an intermediary instead of directly computing red to blue? The answer is, particularly with the Emir image, and I found that changing from red to green, green to blue, instead of doing red to blue, did not significantly change the transformation in other images, but it significantly helped the matching and alignment in the Emir image. In the Emir image, we see that he's wearing a dark blue robe with many different colored features on it. So it has a lot of different features in terms of intensities that shows up differently between the red, green, and blue glass panes. Even though NCC will normalize the brightness difference and differences in contrast between the image, due to the color of the robe and due to the variety of colored embroideries on his robe, we see that it produces very different patterns entirely between the different glass panes. So the normalization of intensity and brightness will not be enough to reconcile these differences in patterns. However, if we look at the individual intensity images for each different color, we see that green is kind of an intermediary in terms of the striking differences between the robe and the embroidered patterns compared to the other two, red and blue. This makes sense as to why computing the alignment for red to green and green to blue will likely perform better than going directly from the stark differences in red directly to blue. Because both NCC and L2 improved with this change, it suggests that this choice of reference color was pretty important here and significantly changes the results.
