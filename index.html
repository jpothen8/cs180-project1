In this project I aim to form the Prokudin-Gorskii glass plate images into a single color image by aligning the captured intensities of the same subject under different colored glass. This wont result in perfect results as there is slight variation between the 3 images, but the goal is to minimize the amount of visual artifacts through strategies below and with trial and error.

Offsets are applied to green and red relative to blue, in (x, y) pixels. Positive x moves right; positive y moves down.

Single-scale alignment

NCC and L2 · search ±15 pixels · scoring border 15%

One, single scaled alignment. Since each scan of the subject contains three grayscale exposures of the same scene under different colored glass panes, the exposures will appear at slightly different positions due to variability in taking the image, and stacking them immediately will cause a lot of visual artifacts in colored outlines or doubled objects. We assume that we can fix these visual artifacts with a translation only, so just moving the entire channel or these images up and down and left and right, and we're not doing any rotation. For this project, we're using a search from negative 15 to positive 15 pixels, so we can shift each channel by this amount. We also exclude the outer 15% of each channel because the borders can sometimes dominate the comparison without looking at the actual important part, which is the scene.

A and B are the reference and shifted patches over the same interior pixels Ω. μ A and μ B are their means; N is the number of pixels.

L2 distance

L2(A, B) = √ ∑ p∈Ω (A p − B p ) 2

Minimize L2. Code score: −L2²/N (maximize).

Two, L2 norm. For this strategy, we will take the L2 norm between the images produced by these different color glass panes, and we'll try to align the image to minimize the intensity difference across each corresponding pixel between these channels. So essentially, we compare green to blue and red to blue separately. For each possible shift, we calculate the mean squared brightness difference between corresponding pixels in the cropped region and choose the shift with the smallest value. This selects the same alignment as minimizing the L2 norm.

Normalized cross-correlation

NCC(A, B) = ∑ p∈Ω (A p − μ A )(B p − μ B ) √ ∑ p∈Ω (A p − μ A ) 2 √ ∑ p∈Ω (B p − μ B ) 2

Maximize NCC.

Three, NCC. With NCC, the main problem we're trying to solve is difference in varying brightness levels across these color channels. So essentially, we're trying to say, can we try to find a metric that will make sure that the patterns across the color channels are as similar as possible? The benefit of this is that it doesn't penalize two color channels for being different just because of their intensity values. For example, if the red channel is the exact same as the green channel in its particular shift, but all of its intensities are doubled, most likely we would want to keep that shift even though the L2 norm will have a high error for that. So how does NCC work? Essentially, we compare the entire cropped image that we are testing against. We subtract the mean brightness of each cropped image, multiply the corresponding mean-centered values, and add them up. We then divide this sum by the product of the two mean-centered images’ L2 norms. Subtracting the means accounts for overall brightness differences, while dividing by the norms accounts for contrast differences. So for example, let's say if we have an array of 10, 20, and 30, and 40, 60, and 80, similar to my example from before, the L2 error will be a lot, but the NCC score will be 1, because after we normalize both these images, we notice that they have the exact same pattern. We start off on a number, and then after demeaning both of these, we get -10, 0, 10, and -20, 0, 20, respectively, which both share the exact same contrast pattern. Because of this, we get the maximum NCC score, which means strong agreement, and because of this normalization, we know that NCC will range from negative 1 to 1.

Multi-scale alignment

Raw-pixel NCC · coarsest side ≤200 pixels · coarse search ±15 · refinement ±2

Four: Pyramid. We run into a problem with this earlier two approaches with larger images because of two reasons. First, we need larger searching areas. We need much more than 15 pixels, and this means that this increases the computational complexity of our searching problem. And then on top of that, our images are also bigger, so finding our loss will also be more computationally expensive. So the core idea is that working with these large images is a pretty big hassle. So what we can do is try to downsample these images into a smaller resolution and then find the best displacement for these downsampled images, and then gradually get back to our original quality, so we can find the exact shift needed for this image. In my project, I create smaller images by applying the kernel [1, 2, 1] divided by 4, both horizontally and vertically, and then keeping every second pixel. Because this is a low-pass filter, it will smooth out fine detail before sampling, and without smoothing, these fine patterns can actually turn into visual artifacts called aliasing. I stop downsampling when the longest side is at most 200 pixels, and we do our first alignment with that lowest resolution image with a plus minus 15 pixel search in that pixel space. As we move to each larger level, we double the previously estimated shift and search within plus or minus 2 pixels of that estimate. We double the shift because each image dimension approximately doubles between levels. The main flaw of this algorithm is that an incorrect coarse estimate at the very beginning can lead later searches into the wrong region, and it may never reach the optimal alignment.

Emir: comparing alignment strategies

Red via green: R→B = R→G + G→B. All four examples use a 15% scoring border and a coarsest side of 200 px.

Seven Emir. In all of the above images, with the same settings for NCC reconstruction, we see here that the Emir robe image shows a pretty big limitation of this brightness-based matching. The color filters can record the same garment or background regions with very different intensities, and since Emir is wearing a dark blue robe with many different colored features on it, it is very hard for the intensities between the red, green, and blue channels to agree with each other, even in the correct alignment. So, in order to play around with this problem a little bit more, I tried different parameters for the alignment. So, for example, I explored both the NCC and L2 metric, borders of 10, 15, and 25%, and the pyramid stopping sizes of 200, 400, and 800. However, the best improvement came from using green as the intermediate reference for aligning each color. If we look at each channel, the differences in the robe is kind of intermediate in the green exposure channel, as it is very dark in the red exposure and very bright or intense in the blue exposure. So, before I was comparing the red with the blue channel and the green to the blue channel, but instead of that, I compare red to green and green to blue. So this red to blue difference is computed by adding these two deltas: red to green and green to blue. This heavily increased the accuracy and alignment in the image, likely because of this fact that green is an intermediate intensity in the robe, which is the main feature that will throw off the traditional alignment in this image. And since both NCC and L2 both improved with this change, it suggests that the reference choice of color was actually pretty important here.
